### Impact of Post‑April 2025 Tariffs on Construction Industry and Procore

In early April 2025, a new wave of tariffs dramatically altered the construction landscape. The United States imposed a **10% baseline tariff on all imports**, with **steeper rates for key trading partners** (e.g. **34% on Chinese imports, 20% on EU imports**) ([Trump installs 10% baseline tariff, country-specific duties | Supply Chain Dive](https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/trump-reciprocal-tariffs-china-japan-eu-vietnam/744301/#:~:text=For%20example%2C%20the%20U,Vietnam%20will%20be%20charged%2046)). These measures (combined with reinstated **25% steel and 10% aluminum duties**) ([Market Watch: How Policy Shifts Are Reshaping Steel Supply  
](https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=24418#:~:text=,United%20States)) have provoked **global retaliatory actions** – from Canada’s counter-tariffs to Europe’s trade defenses – creating **short-term shocks and long-term adjustments** in construction markets worldwide.

- **Regional impacts** (U.S., Canada, Europe, Middle East, Australia/NZ) in the short and long run, including material costs, supply chain disruptions, and trade actions.
    
    - **United States**
        
        - **Short-Term Impacts:** U.S. builders are grappling with **spiking material costs and procurement (funding) volatility**. Essential inputs like steel and aluminum now carry hefty import fees ([25% and 10% tariffs, respectively](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/trump-tariffs-construction-risk-liberation-day/744334/#:~:text=will%20be%20exempted%20from%20these,at%20Associated%20Builders%20and%20Contractors)), which has already driven domestic **[steel prices (US hot-rolled coil (HRC)) ~15% higher in early 2025](https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=24418#:~:text=The%20impact%20on%20pricing%20has,rise%20by%20%24450%20to%20%24600)** . ==Many contractors rushed to== ==**pre-buy materials**====, with== ==[nonresidential construction input prices jumping at a](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/trump-tariffs-construction-risk-liberation-day/744334/#:~:text=Nonresidential%20materials%20prices%20jumped%20at,February%202020%2C%20according%20to%20ABC)== ==**[9% annualized rate in Jan–Feb 2025](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/trump-tariffs-construction-risk-liberation-day/744334/#:~:text=Nonresidential%20materials%20prices%20jumped%20at,February%202020%2C%20according%20to%20ABC)**====.== Ongoing projects continue ([February nonresidential spending hit a record $1.26 trillion](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/trump-tariffs-construction-risk-liberation-day/744334/#:~:text=Nonresidential%20construction%20spending%20hit%20%241,begin%20to%20fade%2C%20said%20Cataldi)), but **new project starts are at risk**. [Developers are hesitating on future work amid cost uncertainty](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/trump-tariffs-construction-risk-liberation-day/744334/#:~:text=Owners%20and%20developers%20of%20commercial,countries%2C%20according%20to%20industry%20sources), and industry economists warn that **“**==**future spending is most at risk”**== ==as current backlogs dry up==. In the public sector, infrastructure projects funded by recent bills carry on for now, but could face **budget overruns** if material inflation isn’t contained.
        
        - **Long-Term Impacts:** Over time, U.S. supply chains will adjust – domestic production of some materials is ramping up ([U.S. steelmakers have announced >$10 billion in capacity investments](https://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=24418#:~:text=The%20impact%20on%20pricing%20has,rise%20by%20%24450%20to%20%24600)), and re-shoring of manufacturing may **bolster construction of new factories**. However, until new capacity comes online, ==**high costs and trade uncertainty may dampen construction demand**==. If tariffs persist, project budgets will need to account for a permanently higher price floor on imports (or find new suppliers). Some relief might come if trading partners negotiate reductions – the White House indicated tariffs could be lowered if others “[remedy non-reciprocal trade arrangements](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/04/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-declares-national-emergency-to-increase-our-competitive-edge-protect-our-sovereignty-and-strengthen-our-national-and-economic-security/#:~:text=,economic%20and%20national%20security%20matters)”. **Regulatory factors** (e.g. immigration policy) compound the challenge: labor shortages could worsen if immigration is tightened, further straining project timelines. In sum, ==the U.S. construction industry may face a== ==**near-term slowdown**== ==(once current projects finish) and== ==**margin pressure**==, followed by a possible rebound if domestic sourcing stabilizes costs and if government stimulus (or trade deals) offsets the drag.
        
    
    - **Canada**
        
        - **Short-Term Impacts:** Canada’s construction sector is caught in the crossfire of U.S. trade policy. The U.S. tariffs (25% on many Canadian goods as of March 2025) were briefly paused for USMCA-compliant products, **sparing key materials like Canadian softwood lumber in the immediate term**. ([Canadian lumber is critical for U.S. homebuilding](https://www.nahb.org/advocacy/top-priorities/building-materials-trade-policy/how-tariffs-impact-home-building#:~:text=Then%20on%20March%206%2C%20Trump,latest%20pause%20on%20tariff%20implementation) – ~72% of U.S. softwood imports – and was exempted from the initial tariff rollout after industry lobbying). Nonetheless, Canadian steel and aluminum exports to the U.S. now face the full 25%/10% duties, reducing their competitiveness. **Domestic Canadian supply** of those metals could increase (as exports divert back home), potentially _l==owering==_ ==local prices for steel and aluminum in Canada in the short run== – a silver lining for Canadian builders. Meanwhile, Canada responded with its own tariffs: **[25% duties on $155 billion of U.S. goods](https://www.nahb.org/advocacy/top-priorities/building-materials-trade-policy/how-tariffs-impact-home-building#:~:text=Then%20on%20March%206%2C%20Trump,latest%20pause%20on%20tariff%20implementation)** (with $30 billion effective immediately in March). These retaliation lists likely target politically sensitive U.S. exports; Canadian firms could see higher costs on certain U.S.-sourced construction inputs (for example, if heavy equipment or building products from the U.S. are now hit with 25% at the Canadian border). However, Canada tried to avoid self-harm – it **considered, then retracted, a tariff on U.S. electricity imports** (which many provinces rely on). Overall, Canadian construction in the short term faces **moderate supply disruptions** (needing to find non-U.S. suppliers for some items) but also an inventory glut for others (excess domestic lumber or steel if U.S. demand dips).
        
        - **Long-Term Impacts:** In the long run, if U.S. protectionism persists, Canada may **pivot to other trade partners**. Canadian steel producers, for instance, might target Europe or Asia to compensate for lost U.S. sales, or scale back production if oversupply drives prices too low. A prolonged U.S.-Canada standoff could also ==**soften Canadian construction demand**== via the broader economy – reduced exports and industrial activity would hurt GDP, and thus commercial and industrial construction investment. On the positive side, Canadian builders might enjoy more stable or even lower prices for certain materials if those remain shut out of the U.S. (creating a buyer’s market domestically). There is also the possibility of **regulatory resolution**: Canada and the U.S. could negotiate sector-specific deals (similar to past quota arrangements on steel/aluminum) to remove uncertainty. Until then, Canadian firms will likely emphasize **cost-saving strategies and local supply development**. Notably, the U.S. has _not_ targeted Canadian lumber under these new tariffs ([relying instead on an existing ~14.5% anti-dumping duty](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/trump-tariffs-construction-risk-liberation-day/744334/#:~:text=will%20be%20exempted%20from%20these,at%20Associated%20Builders%20and%20Contractors)), so Canada’s important forestry sector remains engaged – but any U.S. move to further restrict lumber would directly hit Canadian residential construction activity. In summary, Canada’s construction outlook depends on how quickly trade tensions ease: a drawn-out dispute means **slower growth and cautious project financing**, whereas an early truce could restore confidence.
        
    
    - **Europe (EU & UK)**
        
        - **Short-Term Impacts:** The EU’s construction industry faces **both the U.S. tariffs and Europe’s own countermeasures**. As of April 2025, **U.S. import duties on EU goods are about 20%** (baseline 10% + additional 10%), which directly affects European exporters of construction materials (e.g. specialty steel products, engineered wood, ceramics) and equipment. More significantly, the U.S. reinstated steel and aluminum tariffs (25%/10%) globally, which **closes off the U.S. market for many EU metal producers**. In response, the EU moved swiftly: [it tightened existing](https://www.reuters.com/markets/europe/eu-commission-proposes-25-counter-tariffs-some-us-imports-document-shows-2025-04-07/#:~:text=In%20addition%20to%20these%20counter,at%20import%20quotas%20for%20aluminium) **[steel import “safeguard” quotas by 15%](https://www.reuters.com/markets/europe/eu-commission-proposes-25-counter-tariffs-some-us-imports-document-shows-2025-04-07/#:~:text=In%20addition%20to%20these%20counter,at%20import%20quotas%20for%20aluminium)** [on April 1](https://www.reuters.com/markets/europe/eu-commission-proposes-25-counter-tariffs-some-us-imports-document-shows-2025-04-07/#:~:text=In%20addition%20to%20these%20counter,at%20import%20quotas%20for%20aluminium) to prevent a flood of cheap steel into Europe (as global producers redirect supply). For Europe’s builders, the immediate impact is a mix of ==**higher input costs and supply-chain adjustments**==: domestic EU steel prices could actually _fall_ initially due to **global oversupply** – China’s steel exports rose 22.6% in 2024, and excess Chinese steel is now seeking buyers in Asia and Europe. This surplus has begun **driving down steel prices in Europe**, which might benefit contractors in the short run. However, Europe’s quota cut means not all that cheap steel can enter; EU-based steelmakers (facing less competition from imports but still high energy costs) are not lowering prices much, aiming to protect margins. **Supply chain disruption** is a concern for specialized materials: any U.S.-EU trade frictions on, say, construction machinery or high-tech components (if the U.S. tariffs expand) could delay projects. In the short term, **ongoing European construction projects will continue** (most had procured materials in advance), but tender prices are rising to factor in tariff-related cost risks.
        
        - **Long-Term Impacts:** Over the longer horizon, Europe’s construction sector may undergo **cost structure changes**. If U.S. tariffs persist, European manufacturers of construction materials might pivot more to domestic and intra-EU markets or pursue opportunities in the Middle East/Africa. **European infrastructure projects** could suffer if steel and aluminum remain pricier due to trade barriers – governments might need to increase project budgets or delay certain plans. On the other hand, **European contractors could benefit from cheaper imports from elsewhere.** Europe faces **mixed dynamics**: _initial relief_ from global oversupply (keeping some input costs manageable) but _ongoing uncertainty_ and potential cost inflation if the trade war deepens. Construction subsectors reliant on exports (e.g. luxury architectural components made in the EU for U.S. projects) could see order cancellations, while domestic-focused segments may prove resilient if internal EU demand holds. Overall, expect Europe to **navigate a delicate balance** of shielding its industries (ensuring its steel producers survive) and supporting its builders (ensuring materials remain available at reasonable cost).
        
    
    - **Middle East**
        
        - **Short-Term Impacts:** The Middle East, particularly the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, does not face targeted U.S. tariffs beyond the **10% baseline rate**. U.S. tariffs globally have altered material flows, and Middle Eastern builders might actually find **some imports cheaper**. For example, ==Chinese steel that can’t easily go to the U.S. or Europe may be diverted to Middle East markets at a discount.== **Supply chain stability** is also a short-term advantage in the region: since the Middle East didn’t retaliate (there are _no major counter-tariffs_ by GCC states on U.S. goods), there’s little risk of disruption for U.S.-sourced items like construction equipment or technology. In summary, the immediate effect in the Middle East is **muted**: ongoing projects (e.g. Dubai’s developments, Saudi mega-projects like NEOM) continue unabated, and in some cases might even see **cost savings from global price trends**.
        
        - **Long-Term Impacts:** The longer-term outlook for Middle Eastern construction will depend on global economic ripple effects. If the U.S.-China/EU trade conflict drags on and **global growth slows**, oil demand could weaken, potentially reducing government revenues in oil-producing countries. That, in turn, might force Gulf states to **scale back ambitious infrastructure spending** in later years.
        
    
    - **Australia & New Zealand**
        
        - **Short-Term Impacts:** Australia and New Zealand, as close U.S. allies, were not singled out for extra tariffs beyond the universal 10% U.S. import tax. This means Australian exports of construction-related goods to the U.S. (like liquefied natural gas modules, some engineered wood, etc.) now face a **10% tariff** (unless exempted by content rules). One notable impact is on Australian metals: historically, Australia exports aluminum and some steel to the U.S. (Australia had been exempt from the original 2018 metal tariffs, but in 2025 the U.S. **ended country exemptions, applying 25% steel tariffs to all countries**). Thus Australian aluminum producers (e.g. Rio Tinto’s smelters) and steelmakers now **lose duty-free access** – some shipments might be redirected to other markets. For Australia/NZ construction, the more important effects are indirect and regional. In short, **short-term construction activity in Australia/NZ continues largely as normal**, with potentially **stabilizing or falling prices for internationally traded materials** balancing out any localized cost upticks.
        
    
    - **Long-Term Impacts:** Over the longer term, Australia’s economy, heavily driven by commodity exports (iron ore, coal, LNG), could experience **mixed effects from the tariff era**. Australia and New Zealand’s construction industries are **less directly impacted** by tariffs, and in fact stand to **gain from lower global material costs** in many cases, though they remain vulnerable to any broader economic downturn stemming from global trade conflicts.

- **Effects on construction subsectors** (residential, commercial, industrial, infrastructure).
    
    - **Residential Construction (Homebuilding)**
        
        Tariffs have a pronounced impact on **housing construction costs** and affordability. **Lumber and wood products**, vital for homebuilding, have been a focal point: ==the U.S. continues to levy a ~14.5% combined tariff on Canadian softwood lumber, which, combined with pandemic-era supply issues, has kept lumber prices elevated.== The new 2025 tariffs add further pressure – for example, imported **fixtures, appliances, and materials** (tiles, plumbing fittings, etc.) now often carry the 10% tariff (and higher if from Europe or China). According to the National Association of Home Builders, builders estimate that recent tariff actions **[increase the cost of a typical new single-family home by about $9,200](https://www.nahb.org/advocacy/top-priorities/building-materials-trade-policy/how-tariffs-impact-home-building#:~:text=The%20cost%20of%20building%20materials,actions%20at%20%249%2C200%20per%20home)**. This cost is usually passed to buyers, worsening housing affordability. In the short term, homebuilders accelerated purchases of materials (to get ahead of price hikes), but margins are being squeezed on fixed-price contracts signed pre-tariffs. **Small and mid-sized homebuilders** are particularly vulnerable, as they have less ability to hedge or bulk-buy inventory.
        
    
    - **Industrial Construction (Manufacturing, Warehouses, Energy)**
        
        - **Manufacturing Facilities:** Tariffs on imported machinery raise costs for building factories, potentially causing delays or redesigns, especially for projects relying on foreign equipment. However, U.S. policy incentives and trade strategy are driving a surge in domestic industrial construction—like steel mills and chip fabs—as part of a long-term push for manufacturing self-sufficiency.
        
        - **Warehouses and Logistics Centers:** These are indirectly hit – higher cost of steel (for structural frames and racks) and insulation might bump construction costs a bit. However, the explosion in e-commerce and companies reconfiguring supply chains (holding more inventory domestically to avoid tariff risk) could actually **boost demand for warehouse construction**.
        
    
    - **Infrastructure Construction (Public Works)**
        
        U.S. infrastructure construction is booming due to **federal funding**, but rising material costs from new tariffs threaten to reduce the actual scope of projects within fixed budgets—for example, building fewer miles of road for the same funding. While essential projects will proceed, future bids may shrink or be delayed as agencies grapple with budget pressures and economic uncertainty.
        
        Bottom line for infrastructure: In the near term, project pipelines remain robust but under financial strain

    - **Customer Acquisition and Retention Trends:** In a tariff-inflated environment, **customer acquisition** for Procore might face headwinds _and_ tailwinds. ==Headwind because some firms may freeze new software spending while in survival mode== – a contractor dealing with razor-thin profits due to tariffs might not prioritize a new software implementation. ==Tailwind because the pain of tariff impacts can be a catalyst for change== – firms urgently need solutions for **cost control, procurement tracking, and productivity**.

    - **Strategic Opportunities for Procore**

In fact, industry studies show that embracing digital tools can significantly improve efficiency – **[71% of contractors using digital platforms reported measurable improvements in project efficiency](https://blog.bluebeam.com/construction-cost-management-2025-strategies-rising-prices-policy-shifts/#:~:text=match%20at%20L206%2071,measurable%20improvement%20in%20project%20efficiency).** Thus, the tariff situation acts as a **marketing point** for Procore: it’s not just a collaboration tool, it’s a **cost-control solution** in a time when cost overruns can make or break a project.

Moreover, remote collaboration features (like Procore’s mobile app for on-site updates) help when logistics are uncertain – if a delivery is delayed, all stakeholders see it in the system and can adapt immediately. In global markets, companies in regions less affected might still adopt Procore seeing how it helped peers navigate the chaos. In summary, the situation **expedites the industry’s modernization**, benefiting leading platforms like Procore.

Procore has already been making inroads in infrastructure; the current environment could accelerate that (for instance, state DOTs might encourage their contractors to use Procore for better reporting).
        
        - **Global Expansion Where Demand for Efficiency is Rising:** Interestingly, the regions Procore targets for expansion (Canada, Europe, Middle East, APAC) are all experiencing the ripple effects of tariffs, which in many cases mean ==**tight construction markets that need efficiency gains**====.== Procore can position itself globally as the partner to navigate economic uncertainty.
        
    
    - Strategic Risks for Procore

If the tariff war induces a recession in one or more key markets, many projects could be canceled or delayed indefinitely. In extreme cases, contraction in the industry could lead to **consolidation among construction firms** (fewer, larger firms controlling market share). While larger firms are generally good clients for Procore, fewer total firms could intensify competition among software providers for enterprise accounts, and any failure by Procore to win key consolidators could lose them a chunk of user base.

Procore might have to invest more in local infrastructure or partnerships to comply.
        
        - **Long Sales Cycle for New Types of Customers:** While infrastructure and public sector projects are an opportunity, ==selling to government bodies can be slow and complex.== The urgency tariffs create might not fully translate to speedier procurement by government owners, who are dealing with their own red tape.

### **Company and Customer Overview**

- Vertical SaaS platform for construction services companies
    
    - Customer base is segmented into 3 main stakeholders:
        
        - **General Contractors (60% of ARR) - The primary managers of a construction project who oversee the entire construction process from start to finish. Key responsibilities are:**
            
            - Developing construction budgets
            
            - Hiring/managing sub-contractors
            
            - Collaborating with architects and design teams
            
            - Keeping the project on time
            
            - Inspecting construction quality
            
            - Ensuring week meets contract specifications
            
            - Managing payments to contractors and vendors
            
        
        - **Specialty Contractors (15% of ARR) - Experts who are focused on specific trades or aspects of construction. They are responsible for completing specific, highly technical tasks, and often work under the general contractor as a sub-contractor, but sometimes work directly with the project owner**
            
            - **Examples include:**
            
            - Electrical and plumbing contractors
            
            - HVAC specialists
            
            - Concrete contractors
            
            - Waterproofing experts
            
            - Roofing contractors
            
        
        - **Owners (25% of ARR) - The individuals/entities who initiate the construction project. They work with architects, engineers, and general contractors to bring the construction project to life. They’re responsible for:**
            
            - Defining the project’s scope
            
            - Approving designs
            
            - Providing funding
            
            - Oversee the direction
            
            - Ensuring the final product meets their specifications
            
        
        - Owners include:
            
            - Corporations, universities, government entities, and commercial and residential real estate developers.

### **Business Model**

Procore generates almost all of its revenues from ==subscription revenue== - Revenue is not segmented, it is a single line item

- Company charges based on an annual fixed fee, with the pricing depending on the number and mix of products, as well as on the ==annual construction volume== contracted to run on their platform
    
    - Meaning, they don’t sell on a per-seat basis - customers can put as many users onto the platform as they need to so they can maximize usage
    

- Land and Expand:
    
    - Landing is based on the product
    
    - Expanding is more influenced by volume, plus some uplift from product expansion

Pricing Models:

1. Annual subscription contracts with construction volume over a one-year period;

1. Multi-year subscription contracts with construction volume measured annually over successive one-year periods;
    
    1. Construction volume is measured annually, meaning it's assessed at the end of each 12 months within the multi-year contract - this allows for better cost flexibility for the underlying customer
        
        1. Ex; If a customer does low volumes in 1 year, the ACV can be adjusted for the next year based on that year’s performance
        
    

1. Pooled volume contracts with fixed flat annual fees based on anticipated construction volume measured over multiple years (typically 2-3 years).
    
    1. Under this model, there is no yearly re-calculation of fees based on actual volume used as customers pay a set annual fee for the duration of the contract
    
    1. This pricing model provides downside protection for the company while potentially limiting upside pricing potential
    

“However, **we do not believe NRR is a key metric due to the impact of pooled volume contracts**”

- “Because NRR does not properly capture our customers’ actual construction volume usage under the pool volume model, we do not believe NRR is the best indicator of our ability to retain and grow our customer base.”

- “Pooled volume contracts may also help these customers secure volume-based price discounts from us at contract inception, as well as allow us to secure larger up-front commitments from these customers.”

- **Our subscriptions typically have a term of one to three years**

### **Value Proposition**

Procore’s value prop is to ==digitize the workflows of an extremely low-tech industry.==

- They enable real-time access to project information, simplify complex workloads, and facilitate communication between the different stakeholders identified above

- **Value Prop to Owners:** ==**By reducing friction that hinders collaboration**==**, Procore’s products can help owners track cost updates, project status, and change orders.**
    
    - **They help owners save significant time and money by providing financial and operational visibility into their projects.**
        
        - **It’s critical for owners’ bottom lines that they remain informed of what work has been completed, when it was completed, and what specifically was built or installed.**
        
    
    - **This information is also important for long-term asset management as the data can help out with better predictive maintenance**
    

- **Value Prop to GCs: General contractors operate with** ==**minimal room for error as their profit margins are extremely thin.**==
    
    - **Inadequate information flows, such as not providing SCs with the latest information, can cause project delays which are extremely costly to their bottom lines, overages, and unfulfilled expectations**
        
        - General contractors frequently perform duplicate data entry in disparate systems and are accustomed to dealing with invoicing errors, information silos, and disconnected point solutions
        
        - Ex) General contractors must collect and consolidate dozens of invoices from their specialty contractors each month before invoicing the owner
        
        - With Procore’s platform, that process can be greatly condensed.
        
    
    - **Additionally, GCs can use Procore’s platform to** ==**help with winning bids because they now have data from previous projects to use in their bids**==
    

- **Value Prop to SCs: Similar to GCs, SCs must effectively track and manage their people, materials, and equipment. SCs need to have the right people, with the right equipment and materials, at the job site at the right time.**
    
    - Specialty contractors frequently experience delays and disruptions in work progress as a result of not having timely access to the most up-to-date information, such as when other stakeholders make changes to project plans or schedules and do not effectively communicate those changes to specialty contractors.
    
    - Ex) When a specialty contractor submits a change order, they typically cease work until the change order is approved by the owner and the decision is communicated back to the specialty contractor, which can take weeks.
    
    - Procore’s products feature tools that allow specialty contractors to leverage accurate, real-time information, reduce unnecessary data entry, visualize productivity trends, document completed work, and get paid the correct amounts faster.

### **The Flywheel Effect**

Procore also has a very strong flywheel at play:

- Ex) An owner wants to start a project and they utilize Procore:
    
    - They then hire a general contractor and state that the GC must use Procore to provide the owner with better visibility into the project, or else they will lose the bid
    
    - As a result, the GC will adopt Procore and begin to utilize it for the project
        
        - When they go to hire the specialty contractors, they may tell the SCs to adopt Procore as well so they can provide the Owner with the visibility they asked for
        
    
    - At the same time, the GCs who adopt Procore may invite other Owners to collaborate in Procore, resulting in the Owner deciding to buy Procore
